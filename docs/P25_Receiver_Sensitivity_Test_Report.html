Software P25 Receiver Sensitivity

A software C4FM decoder, fed from a commodity receiver's detector output, measured against the 5% BER reference sensitivity — and compared to a purpose-built P25 radio.
P25 Phase 1 (C4FM) · 159.2475 MHz · VIAVI 8800SX reference generator · 2026-11-25 · receive-only / read-only
Result
5% BER sensitivity ≈ −119 dBm
Within 1–2 dB of a purpose-built commercial P25 radio (−120 to −121 dBm). The measured gap is the receiver front end, not the software. Both converge on the same ≈ −120 dBm thermal noise floor — the physical limit no receiver crosses.

1Objective & claim

This project decodes P25 Tier-1 GPS location straight off the air from a receiver's discriminator / detector audio — no SDR, no cooperating base radio, no vendor software. A fair question follows: how good is the receiver? A radio's receive quality is specified as sensitivity at 5% BER — the weakest signal at which it still recovers bits with no more than 5% in error. This report measures that number for the full capture-and-decode chain and compares it to a radio engineered specifically for P25.

Claim, stated conservatively. At the raw, pre-FEC channel level, a flat USB audio interface on a generic radio's detector pin, decoded entirely in software, reaches 5% BER at ≈ −119 dBm — essentially matching purpose-built P25 hardware. We do not claim to beat it at this layer; we claim to meet it, and we show honestly where the small remaining gap lives.

3Method — how BER is scored

BER is errored bits ÷ total bits, as a percent. To count errors you must know what was supposed to arrive, so the generator sends a defined, deterministic pattern. The sweep pattern is STD 511 / O.153 — a 511-bit pseudo-random sequence (PRBS-9). It exercises all four C4FM symbols evenly (verified: level occupancy 25.0 / 24.9 / 25.1 / 25.0 %), so it stresses every symbol transition the way real traffic would, and it repeats deterministically so a clean reference can be recovered from a strong capture.

Scoring rule (important for rigor). Each weak capture's recovered symbols are aligned to the clean 511 pattern recovered at −90 dBm (a strong, essentially error-free reference), and every symbol/bit deviation is counted across the whole capture. This is immune to the two failure modes that bite a naïve "fold the capture on itself" method near the cliff (§6). ~100,000 bits are scored per level — thousands of error events at the 5% point, a statistically tight estimate.

STD CAL verified structurally as well: the errored symbols fall exactly every 10th dibit (= every 20th bit), matching the pattern's definition precisely — not a coincidental rate match.

6Methodological corrections (full disclosure)

Two artifacts appeared during the sweep and were corrected. Both are documented here because the final number is only trustworthy if the path to it is transparent.

(a) A key-up transient must be trimmed before scoring. The first ~1 second of the −118 dBm capture read 68.8% error — a total loss of lock while the generator settled after the level change — while the rest of the capture read ~5%. Left in, that dirty lead-in inflated the −118 point to 7.2% and produced a false early 5% crossing at −117.6 dBm. Trimming a 1.5 s lead-in (standard BER practice — exclude the acquisition transient) gave the true −118 value of 2.89% and the correct −119 crossing. A per-second error profile is used to confirm each accepted capture is uniform, not transient-contaminated.
(b) Self-referential scoring mislocks at high error rates. An early method that folded each capture on its own repeating period and majority-voted a reference worked at low BER (it agreed with the reference method to 0.001% at −112 dBm) but, near the cliff, its period search locked onto a 5× harmonic with too few repeats and under-reported −119 dBm as 4.33% — non-monotonically below −118. Scoring every capture against the fixed −90 dBm clean reference (§3) is immune to this and restores a monotonic curve. Below the cliff (−120 dBm and beyond) the demodulator itself drops in and out of lock, so points there are noisy by nature and are used only to confirm "well past 5%," not as precise values.

Net effect: the raw sweep is honest and monotonic once the acquisition transient is excluded and scoring is done against a fixed clean reference. The reported −119.1 dBm crossing is bracketed tightly by the −119 (4.35%) and −120 (13.5%) points.

7Comparison to a purpose-built P25 radio

A reference commercial P25 portable, measured consistently across a large field population (on the order of a thousand units, same class of generator and procedure), sits at a 5% BER sensitivity of −120 to −121 dBm. Our software chain reaches 5% at ≈ −119 dBm.

Where the 1–2 dB lives — honestly. The number is read off an NX-700, a generic NXDN/analog radio, whose detector output is DSP-routed and not a front end tuned for C4FM. A purpose-built P25 radio's receiver is tuned for it. The software symbol recovery (MMSE) is already near the theoretical optimum, so the gap is in the radio ahead of the sound card, not the software behind it. A P25-native receiver would likely shrink the gap — but the entire value of the method is that it does not require one.

Everybody meets at the cliff. Thermal noise sets a hard floor at roughly −120 dBm in a 12.5 kHz channel (kTB plus a few dB of receiver noise figure). No receiver — a million-dollar design or a sound card on a detector pin — decodes below it; they all converge there. A 1–2 dB spread between receivers is a noise-figure difference at the front end, not a difference in who "solved" P25. We walked up to the same wall the purpose-built radios stop at. That is the result: not beating physics, but reaching it with commodity parts and software.

8Raw vs corrected — and the next lever

Everything above is raw, pre-FEC channel BER — symbol errors straight off the demodulator, which is exactly how receiver sensitivity is specified, and the honest apples-to-apples contest. But the mission is not raw symbols; it is decoding a GPS fix, and that payload rides forward error correction.

The reference radio decodes that FEC with a conventional hard-decision algorithm. This software decoder can apply soft-decision Viterbi decoding — carrying each symbol's analog confidence into the error correction — which a controlled simulation on the exact P25 code shows yields ≈ 3 dB of coding gain on the GPS data frames. That gain operates on the message layer, where the reference radio leaves it on the table.

The arithmetic worth testing next. −1 to −2 dB (front-end deficit, raw layer) + ≈ 3 dB (soft-decision FEC, message layer) ≈ a net 1–2 dB advantage on actual GPS-fix recovery. Not yet proven — it is the next measurement (a radio at known coordinates swept through the calibrated attenuator, hard-vs-soft frame-success at each level) — but the physics lines up for the software to out-range a purpose-built radio on the thing that matters: plotting a position. Raw symbol layer, we match them; message layer, we have a real shot at beating them.

9Conclusion

A P25 Phase-1 C4FM receiver built entirely from a commodity USB audio interface on a generic radio's detector output, decoded in software, achieves a 5% BER reference sensitivity of ≈ −119 dBm — within 1–2 dB of a purpose-built commercial P25 radio, with the gap attributable to the receiver front end rather than the decoding. At the physical layer the system is essentially noise-floor-limited, the same limit the purpose-built radios reach. The remaining opportunity is not sensitivity (the floor is fixed and already reached) but coding efficiency — soft-decision FEC on the message layer — where a software decoder with real compute can do more with the symbols already captured.

10Reproducibility

  • Decode chain (decode/): frontend.py (RMS-AGC + OP25 C4FM receive filter) → fsk4_demod.py (MMSE symbol tracking) → adaptive 4-level slice to dibits.
  • Scoring (decode/ber_meter.py): self-consistency period-fold for the 1011/baseline and CAL calibration; for the sensitivity sweep, align recovered symbols to the −90 dBm clean 511 pattern and count errored bits over the whole capture (after a 1.5 s lead-in trim).
  • Capture format: 48 kHz / 16-bit / mono PCM WAV, ~10 s per level (≈100 pattern repeats / ≈100k bits). Label each file with its RF level.
  • Patterns: STD 1011 (control), STD CAL (meter calibration), STD 511 / O.153 (sweep). Pattern definitions and purposes: docs/P25_RX_Test_Patterns.pdf.
  • Procedure: validate the meter (synthetic + CAL + 1011 at −90) → sweep STD 511 down in 1 dB steps through the shoulder → bracket the 5% crossing → confirm each accepted capture is transient-free via its per-second error profile.
