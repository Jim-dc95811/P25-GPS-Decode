From Thin Air

What we set out to do

Every modern public-safety radio has a GPS chip in it. It knows its own latitude and longitude to a few feet. The problem has never been knowing the location — the problem is getting it out, to a map, where a commander can see where every crew is.

Today there are only two ways to see those positions. One: pay the radio manufacturer for their whole location system — their radios, their servers, their software, their rules. Two: have one special "base" radio physically wired to a computer, which unwraps the positions and hands them over. Jim's earlier work — The Lookout and the P25 GPS Tracker — used that second path, leaning on a cooperating base radio that de-frames the GPS and squirts it out over a USB cable.

This project removes that dependency entirely. The goal, in Jim's words: "break free from the BK base into the entire world of P25." If we can pull the location out of the radio signal itself, off the air, then any fleet, from any manufacturer, becomes visible from nothing more than a cheap receiver and a sound card. No cooperation. No permission. No cloud.

In plain terms
We are not asking the radio for anything. We are listening to the same transmission any scanner hears, and doing in software what a $10,000 dispatch console does — pulling the GPS out of it. The radio never knows we're there; we only ever receive.

Why it's even possible

P25 location rides on an open, published standard — Tier 1 Location Services (the industry spec TIA-102.BAJB-A). The position is carried as a plain line of NMEA text — the same universal GPS sentence format a boat plotter or a phone uses — tucked inside a P25 data message. That last word matters: it is data, not voice. Decoding P25 voice requires a patented vocoder chip (the part that turns digital back into speech). Decoding data needs none of that. We throw the voice away and keep the data, so there is no vocoder, no patent, nothing proprietary in our path.

NMEA, plainly
A GPS position written as text looks like this: $GPGGA,211707.00,3023.6294,N,08156.2588,W,1,12,0.68,29.6,M,…*4A. That one line says: at 21:17:07 UTC, I'm at 30°23.6294′ North, 081°56.2588′ West, with 12 satellites. Every GPS device on earth speaks this. Our whole job is to recover that one line from a radio burst.
The honesty hook that made it provable
Jim had a secret weapon for proving it: his BK base also hands over the finished NMEA for the very same transmission, over its cable. So we always had the right answer in hand. Decoding the air then became a known-plaintext attack — decode the signal, and diff our answer against the base's, letter for letter. The test was not "does it look right," it was "is it identical: same position and same radio ID." Nothing to fool ourselves with.

What is actually flying through the air

To reverse something, you first have to know how it was built. Here is the journey one position takes, from the radio's GPS chip out into the air — and the mirror-image path we run to get it back.

Walking the top row: the GPS chip produces a position; the radio writes it as an NMEA text line and stamps it with its own individual ID (every radio has a unique number — Jim's fleet runs IDs like 9076802); it wraps that text in a P25 data packet with error-protection around it; it converts the packet's bits into a pattern of four radio tones called C4FM; and it transmits. Our job is the bottom row — undo all four steps.

C4FM and "symbols," plainly
Digital radio can't send 1s and 0s directly — it sends tones. P25 uses four tones (nudging the frequency up a lot, up a little, down a little, down a lot). Each tone carries two bits, so one tone = one symbol (also called a dibit, "two bits"). The radio sends 4,800 of these tones every second. Reading the message means figuring out, 4,800 times a second, which of the four tones is on the air right now.

Where we listen — and why no special radio is needed

Here is the quiet trick at the center of this whole project. Inside any FM receiver — a scanner, a mobile radio, a handheld — there is a point, right after the radio "demodulates" the signal, where the recovered information exists as a plain wobbling voltage. That voltage is the four-tone C4FM pattern, before the radio does anything digital with it. It is called the discriminator output, or detector audio. On a Kenwood NX-700 it comes out on pin 19 of the accessory connector (the "DEO" pin); on a BK portable it's a test point inside.

That wobble is exactly what we need. We feed it into a PC sound card — the same jack you'd plug a microphone into — and the sound card turns the wobble into a stream of numbers, 48,000 of them per second. From there it's all software.

Why this matters — and what an "SDR" is
Almost everyone who decodes P25 uses a software-defined radio (SDR) — a special gadget that captures raw radio waves and does the demodulation itself in software. We use no SDR. We let an ordinary radio do the hard RF work it was built to do, and we tap the audio it already produces. A $20 sound card replaces a radio lab. That is a big part of what makes this cheap, simple, and copyable.
Sound-card settings that matter
Capture in mono, 16-bit, at 48,000 samples per second. 48k is better than 44.1k here because it gives a clean, whole 10 samples per tone (48000 ÷ 4800 = 10 exactly), so nothing has to be resampled. Set the radio to WIDE and leave the squelch open. Turn off every Windows "audio enhancement." The tap is detector audio, not the speaker audio — speaker audio has been filtered for your ears and the fine detail is gone.

Turning the wobble back into a sentence

This is the heart of it — the chain that takes 48,000 numbers a second and gives back one line of GPS text. It runs in stages, each undoing one thing the radio did. Below, each stage in plain terms, then the precise fact.

Stage 1 — Finding the burst, by listening for silence

In plain terms: a GPS message only lasts about half a second, and they arrive maybe once every ten seconds. The rest of the time the channel is just noise. We shouldn't run the expensive decoding on noise — we need a cheap way to notice when a burst starts. The insight that cracked this (Jim's) is counter-intuitive: on an open radio channel, the noise is loud and hissy, and when a signal captures the receiver, the hiss suddenly drops. This is called FM quieting. A P25 burst is a hole in the noise, not a spike in it.

Precisely: we measure the high-frequency "hiss energy" of the audio with a dirt-cheap calculation (the average squared change from one sample to the next — about three operations per sample) and open the gate when that energy falls far below the running noise floor. Idle through hours of dead air costs almost nothing. An older approach that tried to measure signal energy continuously overwhelmed the little router instantly; watching for the quiet is what let the whole thing run on a $40 box.

A landmine we paid for once
When the gate opens, we must hand the decoder a fixed-length slice of audio measured from the burst's start (we grab from 0.35 s before the onset through 1.4 s total). An earlier version cut the slice when the "quiet" ended — which chopped the burst in half and garbled the result. The rule: once you find the start, take a fixed window; never trust the end.

Stage 2 — Reading the four tones (the "eye")

In plain terms: the four tones don't arrive crisp. They're smeared together, they drift in loudness, and the whole thing wanders slowly up and down (a DC drift). To tell the four levels apart cleanly, the software has to lock onto three things at once: the rhythm (when exactly each tone starts), the loudness (so the four levels sit where expected), and the drift (cancel the slow wander). When it locks, the four levels separate into clean bands — engineers call that clean picture an eye diagram, because the gap between bands looks like an open eye.

Precisely: this is a faithful port of OP25's tracking_loop_mmse symbol recovery. A naïve fixed ruler gave about 15% tone errors — hopeless. The tracking loop uses a 129×8 interpolation table to find the exact sub-sample timing of each tone, an automatic level control to hold the four levels at their nominals, and a slow integrator to null residual DC once it locks. Then each tone is sliced at fixed thresholds into its two bits.

The single biggest mistake of the whole build — and the fix
Early on, the tones decoded badly and there was an odd distortion right at each burst's start. I blamed the capture hardware — a "DC transient" from the radio. I was wrong. The real cause was a crude filter I had hand-written and bolted onto the front of the chain. The captures were always clean. Deleting my filter and dropping in OP25's exact, proven front-end filter is what made the whole thing fall out correctly. Lesson, earned twice this project: when the hardware hands you a stable, correct signal, suspect your own code, not the radio.

Stage 3 — Finding where the message begins (frame sync)

In plain terms: we now have a river of bits, but we don't know where a message starts. P25 solves this the way a telegraph does: every message begins with a fixed, agreed-upon magic pattern. We slide along the bit-stream until we see that pattern, and now we know we're aligned.

Precisely: the P25 frame sync is the 48-bit word 0x5575F5FF77FF. We shift each tone into a running accumulator and declare a match when it differs from the magic word in at most 6 bit positions (its Hamming distance — simply the count of disagreeing bits — is ≤ 6, which tolerates a few tone errors without missing a real sync).

Stage 4 — Reading the address label (the NID)

In plain terms: right after the start marker comes a small header that says which radio system this is and what kind of message follows. It's protected by an error-correcting code so a few flipped bits can be repaired, not just detected.

Precisely: the NID (Network ID) is read from the frame bits, skipping two "status" bits the radio periodically injects, and decoded with a BCH error-correcting code — accepting the result only if ≤ 4 bit-errors were corrected. It yields the NAC (the system's color-code; Jim's reads 0x455) and the DUID (the message type; 0xC means a Packet Data Unit — exactly what carries GPS).

Stage 5 — The payload, and fixing its errors (the win)

In plain terms: now the actual GPS text, chopped into protected blocks. P25 scrambles and error-codes the data so it survives a noisy channel. Unscrambling is routine; the error-correction is where the project was nearly lost — and then won.

Precisely: Tier-1 location rides in an Unconfirmed Data Packet, Location Service (the packet header is format 0x15, service access point 0x30) — direct NMEA, not the internet-style framing some systems use. Its data blocks are protected by a rate-1/2 convolutional code. OP25's built-in decoder walks that code greedily — picking the best-looking choice symbol by symbol — and hard-fails on a tie. A few stray tone errors scattered through a block create a tie, and the whole block is lost. On Jim's capture, greedy recovered only 4 of 12 blocks — no usable sentence.

The decode win
A convolutional code's correct decoder is not greedy — it's the Viterbi algorithm, which, instead of guessing each step, finds the single most-likely whole path through the data at once. Think of reading a smudged sentence: rather than forcing each letter in isolation, you pick the most likely sentence that fits all the smudges together. We added a proper Viterbi decoder for the 1/2-rate blocks (the same kind of optimal decoder OP25 already uses elsewhere). It recovered 12 of 12 blocks — the complete sentence — where greedy got 4. That single change turned a pile of fragments into a clean position.

Out of this stage comes the GPS text and the transmitting radio's ID — and a lovely, unforgeable detail: the block header carries the radio's own address, which for Jim's unit reads 8A 80 42 = 9076802. The decode isn't just producing a position — it's proving whose radio it read.

Proving it was real

This is where the answer key earned its keep. For each burst, we lined our over-the-air decode up against the BK base's own NMEA for the identical transmission:

# our decode, straight off the air: OTA: $GNGGA,174713.00,3023.63049,N,08156.25802,W,1,08,1.47,24.5,M,-31.1,M,,*4D $PP25,9076802,*35 # the base's answer key, same transmission: KEY: $GNGGA,174713.00,3023.63049,N,08156.25802,W,1,08,1.47,24.5,M,-31.1,M,,*4D $PP25,9076802,*35

Identical — the time, the latitude and longitude, the satellite count, the altitude, the checksum, and the radio-ID line. Every field. On a clean capture of 10 bursts, we decoded 10 of 10, with position and unit ID exact on every one. Two independent, unforgeable facts per burst: where and which radio. That is proof, not a hunch.

The honest caveat we kept
An earlier, rougher capture decoded only 6 of 11 bursts. We chased it and found the misses were a signal problem, not a decoder problem: a contiguous ~30-second stretch of that recording was genuinely degraded (every burst before and after it decoded perfectly). A clean capture clears them, which the 10/10 run confirmed. We never papered over the misses; we proved their cause.

The agents, the dead ends, the honest road

This was not a straight line, and it was not one mind. The decode was built with a team of agents working the problem in parallel, and the path was littered with wrong turns we kept — because the wrong turns are the map for the next person.

  • The search-first rule paid the rent. Rather than hand-roll a P25 decoder from scratch, we stood on the open-source OP25 project (and its forks). The hard, proven DSP and error-correction math is OP25's, ported verbatim. Our original contributions are narrow and surgical: the noise-quieting squelch, the Viterbi step, the acquisition logic, and everything that put it on a box.
  • The hand-rolled filter. My own front-end filter was the villain behind the "hardware transient" (above). Replacing it with OP25's real filter was the turning point.
  • Greedy vs. Viterbi. Accepting OP25's greedy block decoder cost us most of the sentence until Viterbi replaced it.
  • Energy detection vs. the noise hole. Trying to find bursts by their energy found nothing (the noise is louder than the signal). Watching for the quiet found every one.
  • The walled sources. Several key P25 references (the OP25 wiki among them) block automated reading. Jim pulled them up in his own browser, saved them to files, and handed them over so they could be read in full. That is how we got the exact slicer table and the frame-sync magic word. Human and agent, hands and eyes, one team.

From a decode on a laptop to a box in a backpack

Decoding on a powerful PC was the first mountain. The real goal was a self-contained appliance — a battery-powered box that does everything itself, with no PC in the field. That meant moving the whole decoder onto a $40 travel router (a GL-MT3000), and it surfaced three hard problems, each worth understanding.

Problem 1 — the box has no math libraries

In plain terms: the PC decoder leaned on two big scientific-math packages (numpy and scipy) that simply don't exist for this little router. So the entire decoder had to be rewritten in plain Python, using nothing but what ships in the language itself.

Precisely: plain Python is perhaps 20× slower than the math libraries, which sounds fatal. It isn't, because of the squelch: the box idles almost for free and only does the heavy work during a ~half-second burst, and bursts are ten seconds apart. Measured, the stdlib decoder runs at roughly half of real-time on the router's ARM chip — comfortably fast enough, with margin.

Problem 2 — Python can only do one heavy thing at a time (the GIL)

In plain terms: Python has a rule (the GIL) that lets only one piece of heavy work run at a time. While the box was busy decoding a burst for a couple of seconds, it stopped emptying the sound card's little bucket of incoming audio — the bucket overflowed, and the next burst or two were lost.

Precisely: the fix is a tiny helper whose only job is to scoop raw bytes out of the sound card's pipe and drop them in a queue — a job so trivial it doesn't hold the GIL. All the real work (and the decode) happens downstream, which now has idle time between bursts to catch up. The pipe stays empty; no overflow; consecutive bursts caught.

Problem 3 — the sound card and the map drive wouldn't share the box

In plain terms: the box has to run three USB things at once — the sound card (to hear the radio), an SSD (to hold the offline maps), and a GPS puck (for the box's own location) — all through one little USB hub. On the router's original software, plugging in the sound card and the map drive together was refused: the system wrongly thought there wasn't enough USB bandwidth for both.

Precisely: this is a known bug in the older Linux kernel (the core software that runs the hardware) — specifically in how the MediaTek USB scheduler accounts for devices behind a hub. Jim refused the cheap workaround (crippling the sound-card quality to fit). The right fix is a newer kernel (6.6) where the bug is repaired. We moved the box to that firmware (OpenWrt 24.10), and all three devices now share the hub cleanly — proven on real hardware. That was the entire hardware risk of the project, and it's gone.

From a position to a map a crew can use

A decoded position is still just text. The last stretch turns it into something a firefighter opens on a phone.

The clever part is a non-event: the box's decoder is built to emit the exact same NMEA the BK base used to send, on the same network port. So the map software — already proven on The Lookout — reads it unchanged. The over-the-air decoder is a drop-in replacement for the cooperating base; the map can't tell the difference. (Along the way we tidy the decoder's output: force the standard GPS prefix and recompute the checksum, so a stray bit in the label can never hand the map a bad line, while the position and ID pass through exactly as decoded.)

The map itself is a locked split-screen — satellite on top, street below, same place — drawn from offline map tiles on the SSD, with the live radio dots and the box's own position riding on both halves. It opens in any browser at the box's address; a printed QR sticker on the box lets a crew scan straight to it.

"You are here" — and a decision worth recording
The box carries its own GPS puck (a Garmin, read directly over USB) so it can show its own location. The Garmin reports a confidence flag with each fix. My code originally refused any fix below the "full lock" level — and threw away a perfectly good, stable, correct position the puck was reporting at a lower confidence, exactly the degraded fix a firefighter gets indoors or under tree cover. Jim caught it. We changed the rule to trust a usable position (rejecting only a fix the GPS itself flags as integrity-failed), because a labeled, imperfect position beats no position when someone needs to be found. The domain expert overruled the arbitrary threshold — correctly.

One button turns a bare box into this

None of it is useful to a crew if standing it up takes an engineer. So the last piece is the Universal Traveler Manager — a PC app that turns a factory-fresh router into a finished Universal Traveler in one click: it checks the box is on the right firmware (and can flash it there), installs the handful of system packages, streams every program onto the box, installs the boot services so everything comes back after a power-cycle, and then actually verifies the map draws and a position is listening — not merely that "a program is running." A box provisioned this way survives being unplugged and powered back on with no one at a keyboard, which we proved with a cold power-pull.

Where it stands — and what it means

As of 23 November 2026 the whole chain is proven, live, on the box: a P25 fleet radio decoded off the air by the box itself and plotted on the offline split-screen map, next to the box's own position, with nothing on the viewing phone — no PC, no cooperating base, no internet.

And the thing worth sitting with: outside the radio manufacturers, there is no public precedent we can find for this. Everyone else decoding P25 off the air uses a software-defined radio and is after voice; the rare location work targets one manufacturer's proprietary format and is an ongoing, partial reverse-engineering effort. Standardized Tier-1 location is so new the industry only recently began writing its formal specification. Pulling that standardized GPS from the air, with no SDR, off plain detector audio, and plotting it live on a self-contained battery box — the public record shows no one has done it this way. We won't claim an absolute first, because we can't see every private lab. But searched and checked: no one has shown this done the way it was just done here.

The point of all of it is small and human. A commander will be able to open a map and see where the crews are — from any P25 fleet, on a box that costs less than dinner, with no permission from anyone. For the people who run into the places everyone else runs out of, being findable is not a feature. It's the whole thing.

The next chapter — the story wasn't over

Everything above this line was written on the night of 23 November 2026, and it called itself "the complete story," finished "the night it finally worked end to end." It was wrong — gloriously, happily wrong. What it proved — a position, pulled off the air, drawn on a map — turned out to be the first half. Two bigger things were already on the way: shedding the whole box, and hearing the fleet.

Read what follows as the second half of the same work, two days on. Nothing above changes; this is where it went.

Off the box, onto any PC — the pure form

Look back at what the box cost us: flashing firmware, talking to it over SSH, the USB-bandwidth fight, rewriting the whole decoder in plain Python because the little router had no math libraries. Every bit of that apparatus existed for one reason — to carry the decode onto a locked-down work PC, the kind an agency freezes so tightly that no one can install Python on it. The box was a way around a wall.

On a normal PC — one you're allowed to run Python on — that wall isn't there, and the machinery melts away. No box to flash, no SSH, no firmware. Just a USB sound card on the radio's detector audio, a USB GPS for your own position, your offline maps in a folder, and one double-click. The same decode, the same map, with none of the scaffolding.

In plain terms
The battery box was never really the point — it was the compromise for a computer you're not allowed to touch. On a computer you are allowed to touch, the whole invention is just a small program and a $20 sound card. So that became the real product; the box lives on as the answer for the locked-down machines, where a program you can't install is useless.

That pure-PC form is the flagship now: Claude McNally's PC P25 Receiver and GPS Map Plotter — free, standalone, with its own name and its own front door. The box (the Universal Traveler) stays as the option for a Group-Policy-locked agency PC. Same decode at the heart of both.

The other half of the signal — hearing it

Go back to the very beginning of this document. We made a deliberate choice there: the transmission carries both data and voice, and we threw the voice away — "no vocoder, no patent, nothing proprietary in our path." That was the clean road to the position. But the voice was still right there, in the same bursts, every time a unit keyed up. The question came back around: could we hear it too — in pure software, with no special hardware?

Everyone says no. P25 voice is squeezed by a vocoder — the part that compresses speech into a tiny digital stream and expands it back to sound — and that vocoder was patented by a company called DVSI. The accepted wisdom is that to hear P25 you must buy their licensed chip (a "DVstick"). We nearly ordered one.

The wall that wasn't there anymore
P25 Phase-1 conventional voice is IMBE 4.4k — and the IMBE patents have expired. The still-encumbered part is the newer AMBE+2 codec (Phase-2 and the like); plain Phase-1 IMBE is now free to implement in software. The dongle everyone buys is decoding, in its Phase-1 mode, a codec whose patents have lapsed. The chip wasn't a law of physics — it was a habit.

So we wrote the vocoder ourselves, in pure Python. It is a faithful port of the open-source mbelib — the same reference every hobbyist decoder leans on — carried over so exactly that we reproduced the C library's own random-number generator step for step. (The vocoder uses randomness to fill the breathy, unvoiced parts of speech; only an identical random stream gives identical sound.) The result matches the reference decoder to better than 0.999 correlation — not "sounds similar," but numerically the same speech.

Then we pointed it at the air. First a bench recording — a voice saying "Testing 1234 1234," three times — came out of our software as exactly that phrase, three times, confirmed by ear. Then live fleet traffic, played back clean through the browser. Jim's words when it worked: "WE ARE IN!!!! WORKS PERFECT!"

What this means, plainly
No dongle. No SDR. No licensed chip. The same sound card that reads the position now also reconstructs the voice, in software that runs anywhere Python does. The two halves of the transmission — where they are and what they said — both come off one wire.

Who is talking — lighting the speaker on the map

Here is the detail that ties the two halves into one picture. Every voice transmission carries, folded into its own control data, the ID of the radio that is talking — and it is the same individual ID the GPS plots by. We pull it out of the voice frames (the Link Control source address) and we know, for each key-up, which unit is on the air.

So the map does something no scanner does: when a unit keys up, its dot lights up. You don't just hear a voice — you see, on your own imagery, exactly which crew it is and where they're standing while they speak.

In plain terms
The voice quietly announces "unit 9076801 is talking" inside every transmission. We read that, match it to the dot already on the map by the same number, and ring it. Where, who, and voice — fused by the radio's own ID.

The combo platter — see them and hear them at once

Put it together, and this is what a commander opens in a browser tab: every unit on their own offline map by its ID; their own position beside them; a Listen button; and, the instant anyone keys up, a Current Speaker readout with that unit lit on the map while the voice plays. One sound card. The whole fleet, located and heard.

It has been proven live — three units on the map at once, the operator's own position and two named crews, with "Jax 25 speaking" lit and ringed while its voice came through the browser, nothing installed on the viewer.

A quiet side-invention
The receiver captures a whole key-up and then plays it, rather than streaming it live as it arrives. On a long transmission that runs a little behind real time — but you hear every word from the first syllable, with no clipped front end and no squelch tail, and on back-and-forth traffic a listener at a distance can't tell it was ever delayed. A cleaner listen than the radio itself gives, as a free consequence of how it's built.
Rule #1 — never feed the vocoder garbage
A vocoder handed a badly-received frame doesn't go quiet — it screeches. So the decoder scores each key-up's quality first and mutes a transmission that came in too broken to be worth playing, instead of blasting noise. Picky, and tunable. The quality is read from error counts the decode already produces, so it costs nothing extra.

As deaf as the radio, and no deafer

One more question deserved a real answer: how weak a signal can this software still read? We measured it properly — driving a calibrated lab signal generator (a VIAVI service monitor) down in level until the decode broke. Our software, off a plain sound card, held to the industry's 5%-error point at about −119 dBm — squarely in the band a purpose-built commercial P25 radio is rated for. The cheap path is not a deaf path. It hears the air about as well as the radio that feeds it does.

The Holy Grail

Step all the way back. Years ago, the idea of getting GPS out of P25 for firefighter safety was raised — and shut down, for reasons that had nothing to do with whether it could be built. It sat as an unfinished want: the original Holy Grail.

It isn't a want anymore, and it isn't a lab result. It is a free, public, standalone product that anyone can download and run — with its own name, its own front page, and a live screenshot of it working as the first thing a visitor sees. It pulls both the position and the voice of any P25 fleet straight out of the air, fuses them by the radio's own ID, and plots and plays them on your own offline map. No SDR. No base station. No internet, no cloud. No licensed chip. No permission from anyone.

The point of all of it stays small and human — and now it's doubled. A commander opens a map and sees where the crews are, and hears them. For the people who run into the places everyone else runs out of, being findable was already the whole thing. Being heard too is the rest of it.

Glossary — every term, plainly

The jargon in this document, in plain words.
TermWhat it means
NMEAThe universal text format for a GPS position, e.g. $GPGGA,…. Every GPS device speaks it. Recovering one NMEA line is the goal.
P25Project 25 — the digital two-way radio standard used by most U.S. public safety agencies.
Tier 1 LocationThe open, published P25 standard (TIA-102.BAJB-A) for carrying GPS position as NMEA over the radio. Any manufacturer can implement it.
Discriminator / detector audioThe raw, demodulated wobble inside any FM receiver — the four-tone pattern before the radio digitizes it. Our tap point. (NX-700 pin 19 "DEO"; BK internal test point.)
SDR (software-defined radio)A device that captures raw radio waves and demodulates in software. What everyone else uses — and what we deliberately do not need.
C4FMThe four-tone modulation P25 uses. Four frequency steps, each carrying two bits.
Symbol / dibitOne of the four tones = one symbol = two bits ("dibit"). P25 sends 4,800 per second.
BaudSymbols per second. P25 is 4,800 baud.
FM quietingThe effect where a captured FM signal makes the receiver's background hiss drop. It's why a burst is a hole in the noise.
SquelchThe gate that decides "signal present." Here, it opens when the hiss goes quiet.
Eye diagramThe picture of cleanly-separated signal levels. An "open eye" means the tones are being read cleanly.
MMSE loopThe symbol-recovery algorithm (from OP25) that locks onto timing, level, and DC drift so the four tones read cleanly.
Frame syncA fixed magic bit-pattern (0x5575F5FF77FF) at the start of every P25 frame, used to find where a message begins.
Hamming distanceThe number of bit positions in which two patterns differ. A small distance to the magic word = a match despite a few errors.
NID / NAC / DUIDThe frame's little header: NID is the whole thing; NAC is the system's color-code (0x455 here); DUID is the message type (0xC = data packet).
BCHAn error-correcting code protecting the header — fixes a few flipped bits, not just detects them.
PDUPacket Data Unit — the data message that carries the GPS text.
Convolutional code / FECForward Error Correction: extra bits woven through the data so the receiver can repair errors. A convolutional code is one common kind.
Viterbi algorithmThe optimal way to decode a convolutional code — finds the single most-likely whole message at once, rather than guessing bit by bit. Our key win (12/12 blocks vs. 4/12 greedy).
Greedy decodeA simpler decoder that picks the best choice at each step and can't recover from a tie. What we replaced with Viterbi.
CRCA checksum that detects whether a block decoded correctly.
Individual IDEach radio's unique number (Jim's fleet: 9076801/02/03…), carried in every position report. Lets us prove which radio we decoded.
Known-plaintext attackProving a decode by comparing it to a trusted copy of the same message (here, the BK base's own NMEA).
RNDISThe USB-networking link the BK base uses to hand over finished NMEA — the "answer key" path we validate against.
OP25The open-source P25 decoder project our DSP and error-correction math is ported from.
numpy / scipyBig scientific-math libraries for Python — fast, but unavailable on the router, which forced the plain-Python rewrite.
GILPython's rule that only one heavy task runs at a time. It caused the audio-overflow bug; the fix was a trivial reader that doesn't hold it.
KernelThe core software that runs a computer's hardware. The USB bug lived in an old kernel; kernel 6.6 fixes it.
OpenWrt / op24The Linux system the router runs. "op24" is the 24.10 / kernel-6.6 firmware where the sound card and SSD coexist.
Leaflet / TPKX tilesThe browser map engine and the offline satellite/street map files it draws from the SSD.
Garmin D800The binary protocol the box's own GPS puck speaks over USB, read directly in software.
VocoderThe part that compresses speech into a tiny digital stream and expands it back to sound. P25's is IMBE/AMBE. We wrote one in pure software.
IMBEThe P25 Phase-1 voice codec (4.4 kbit/s). Its patents have expired, which is why a free-software decode is legal.
AMBE+2 / DVSIThe newer, still-patented codec (Phase-2 and others) from DVSI, the vocoder company. The licensed "DVstick" is for this — not needed for Phase-1 IMBE.
mbelibThe open-source reference vocoder library our pure-Python IMBE decoder is faithfully ported from.
LDUA P25 voice frame (Logical Data Unit) carrying the compressed speech — and, in the first of each pair, the talking radio's Link Control.
Link Control / source IDControl data inside the voice frames naming the talking radio — the same individual ID the GPS plots by. It's what lights the current speaker on the map.
Current SpeakerThe live readout of which unit is transmitting, taken from the Link Control source ID and shown by lighting that unit's dot.
dBm / sensitivityA measure of signal power (more negative = weaker). "Sensitivity" is the weakest signal still decodable; ours is ≈ −119 dBm, matching a commercial P25 radio.

P25-GPS-Decode · the story through 2026-11-25. Receive-only throughout — the radio is never transmitted to or altered. The signal-processing and error-correction stages are ported from the open-source OP25 project (osmocom / boatbod / and forks); the IMBE voice decoder is ported from the open-source mbelib; the noise-quieting squelch, the Viterbi 1/2-rate decoder, the acquisition logic, the current-speaker fusion, and the appliance and PC products are original to this work. Every over-the-air position was validated character-for-character against a cooperating base's own NMEA; every decoded voice was confirmed by ear.
