From discriminator audio to a plotted position — the complete signal chain, explained.
What this is Project 25 Tier 1 Location Service moves a unit's GPS fix over the air as ordinary NMEA text wrapped inside a P25 data packet — no dispatch infrastructure, no internet, no cooperation from the base station required to read it. This document reconstructs the entire operation end to end: how the fix is formatted, how it is error-protected and modulated, where it can be tapped out of a receiver, and exactly how to turn that recovered baseband back into latitude and longitude. It is written to stand on its own — you should be able to hand it to an engineer and have them build the decoder from it.

Provenance & scope

No single published document describes this operation whole. The physical layer lives in the P25 Common Air Interface specifications; the data-packet machinery lives in the P25 Data standards; the location payload lives in the Tier 1 Location Services specification; the receiver's tap point lives in a radio's service manual; and the only working reference code lives inside open-source decoders written for an entirely different purpose (voice and text, over software-defined radio). This document assembles those pieces into one coherent engineering narrative and expands on each — the standards tell you what; this tells you how, and why. It is an independent educational synthesis in our own words and original figures; it does not reproduce any copyrighted standard, and the numeric values it cites are the functional facts an implementer needs, not transcriptions.

The one idea to carry through the whole document
A Tier 1 location report is NMEA you already know, hidden under error-correction and modulation you can undo. There is no encryption on the data, no proprietary compression, and no secret format. Every layer between the GPS sentence and the RF is a public, deterministic transform. Strip the transforms in the right order and the latitude and longitude fall out as plain ASCII text.

The problem, and the shape of the answer

A firefighter keys up a portable radio. Two hundred milliseconds of digital chatter rides out on the channel alongside the voice traffic. Inside those milliseconds is the radio's own GPS fix. The question this document answers is: how do you get that fix onto a map without asking anyone's permission?

Public-safety radio location has historically been a walled garden. The position data exists — modern P25 portables know where they are — but reading it usually means buying into a vendor's dispatch system, a server, a subscription, and a chain of cooperation that a small or underfunded agency may never get. The irony is that the data itself is standardized and open on the air, precisely so that radios from different manufacturers can interoperate. The lock is on the infrastructure, not the signal.

Project 25 defines location services in two tiers. Understanding the split is the first move, because it tells you which problem you are solving:

  • Tier 2 is the infrastructure tier. Position is carried as IP datagrams through the fixed network, with request/response protocols, triggered reporting, and server-side hosts. In the field this is the realm of vendor protocols such as Motorola's LRRP. It is powerful, heavy, and not what we want.
  • Tier 1 is the field tier. It is deliberately the simple case: one radio sends its NMEA fix directly to another radio (or through a repeater) over the Common Air Interface, with no IP addressing, no fixed-network routing, and no advanced triggering. It exists for exactly the scenario we care about — a portable talking to a portable (or to a laptop acting as a display) at an incident.

Tier 1 is the door that was left unlocked. Its payload is NMEA 0183 — the same GPS sentence format that pours out of every marine plotter and USB puck on earth — carried inside a plain, unconfirmed P25 data packet. Nothing in that chain is secret. The rest of this document is the anatomy of that chain and the recipe for walking back up it.

System architecture — who talks to whom

Before touching bits, fix the players in your head. Tier 1 names four roles. Three of them matter to us, and two of them are things you already own.

  • LIS — Location Information System. Whatever tells the radio where it is: the GPS receiver chip. Usually internal to the portable. Not our concern except that it is the origin of the fix.
  • SU — Subscriber Unit. The radio. The sending SU takes the fix from its GPS and transmits it; the receiving SU pulls it off the air and hands it to a display. In our world the sending SU is a field portable; the receiving SU is the base radio on the bench.
  • FSR — Fixed Station Repeater. Optional. If the two radios cannot hear each other directly, a repeater relays the packet. To the data, a repeater is transparent — same bytes in, same bytes out — so it changes nothing about decoding.
  • LSHS — Location Service Host System. The thing that displays the fix on a map. The standard explicitly notes this is typically a commercial off-the-shelf product — a laptop running mapping software. This is the role our decoder plays. We are a legitimate, standards-anticipated LSHS; we simply source the fix from the air rather than from a vendor's cable.

Two physical configurations are defined, and both are in scope:

  • Direct Data: SU → SU. The sending radio transmits; the receiving radio hears it directly.
  • Repeated Data: SU → FSR → SU. Same thing through a repeater.
Why the open interfaces matter
The standard refuses to specify the LIS→SU and SU→LSHS interfaces, reasoning that a GPS chip or a map application may be built right into the radio, and that the location industry evolves faster than a frozen interface would allow. That refusal is a gift: it means reading the fix at the receiving SU's output and plotting it yourself is not a workaround — it is the LSHS role operating exactly as intended.

The physical layer — C4FM and the four levels

Everything on a P25 Phase 1 channel is carried by C4FM: continuous-phase four-level frequency modulation. Strip away the acronym and it is simple — the transmitter shifts its frequency to one of four values, 4800 times a second, and each of those four values stands for two bits.

The C4FM symbol alphabet. Four frequency deviations, each carrying one dibit (two bits).
DibitSymbolFrequency deviationDiscriminator level
01+3+1800 Hzouter high
00+1+600 Hzinner high
10−1−600 Hzinner low
11−3−1800 Hzouter low

The numbers drive the whole front end, so internalize them:

  • 4800 symbols per second. Each symbol is two bits, so the gross bit rate is 9600 bps. One symbol lasts 208.3 µs.
  • Four levels, symmetric about zero. The deviations are ±1800 Hz and ±600 Hz — an outer pair and an inner pair, mirror-imaged around the carrier. A frequency discriminator turns frequency deviation into voltage, so on its output those four frequencies become four voltage levels, symmetric about zero.
  • A 3:1 ratio. The outer level is three times the inner level. That fixed geometry is how a slicer sets its thresholds without knowing the absolute gain of the radio: find the extremes, and the inner levels sit at ±⅓ of the way out.

Why four-level FM rather than a phase-shift scheme? Two reasons the P25 designers cared about. First, a constant-envelope FM signal lets a transmitter run its power amplifier hard and cheap without spectral regrowth. Second — and this is the part that helps us — the receiver that demodulates C4FM is an ordinary FM discriminator, the same block that has lived in two-way radios for decades. P25 Phase 1 was deliberately designed so that a single C4FM/π4-DQPSK receiver can demodulate it. The consequence for a decoder builder is enormous: by the time the signal reaches the discriminator output, it is already demodulated. We never touch RF.

The waveform does not look like clean steps because the signal is deliberately band-limited (a shaping filter keeps it inside the 12.5 kHz channel) and because the receiver's audio path rounds the edges further. This is fine. A slicer does not read the slopes; it reads the centers. If you fold many symbols on top of one another at the symbol rate, the centers stack into four tight horizontal bands separated by open gaps — the classic eye diagram. A clean eye with four open bands is the single best proof that a capture is good before you decode a single bit.

The tap — getting baseband out of a receiver

There are two honest ways to obtain C4FM baseband: demodulate it yourself from RF with a software-defined radio, or take it from a receiver that has already demodulated it. The second is simpler, cheaper, and more robust for a fixed-channel job — and it is the path this document assumes.

A conventional dual-conversion FM receiver takes the antenna signal down through two intermediate frequencies, limits it, and runs it into a frequency discriminator — the block that converts frequency deviation into an audio-band voltage. In a digital radio that recovered baseband (often labeled DISC AUD or "discriminator audio") normally flows onward to a codec and DSP. The tap is simply a connection to that recovered baseband before the radio's own DSP consumes it.

Capturing it cleanly

The capture device is a PC sound card. Two practical points decide whether the capture is usable:

  • Sample rate. At 96 kHz you get roughly twenty samples per symbol — more than enough to reconstruct the waveform and find the symbol centers. 48 kHz (ten samples/symbol) also works. Record mono.
  • Baseline wander — the one real trap. Most sound-card line inputs are AC-coupled: they pass only changing signals and slowly drift a constant level back toward zero. But C4FM carries information in absolute level, and a run of identical symbols is a constant level. Over a long run the coupling lets that level sag, smearing the four bands. The fix is twofold: keep bursts short (a GPS packet is only a couple hundred milliseconds, so wander is limited) and restore the baseline in software by continuously tracking the signal's running center and re-referencing the slicer to it. A DC-coupled interface avoids the problem entirely but is rarely necessary for short packets.

Finding the packet in the noise — sync and framing

A raw discriminator recording of an idle channel is full-scale hiss. When a transmission begins, that hiss collapses into the orderly four-level structure of C4FM. That transition — noise to structure — is itself a reliable trigger: a simple measure of signal variance, or energy at the 4800 Hz symbol rate, marks where each burst starts and ends. You can segment every packet out of a long recording automatically, with no hand-trimming.

Segmenting the burst is not the same as knowing where the frame begins inside it. For that, P25 puts a fixed marker at the head of every frame:

  • Frame Synchronization (FS): a fixed 48-bit (24-dibit) pattern, 0x5575F5FF77FF, identical on every P25 Phase 1 frame. You locate it by correlation — slide the known pattern across the recovered symbol stream and watch for the spike where they line up. That spike is an exact, unambiguous ruler: everything after it is measured from a known origin.
  • Network Identifier (NID): the 64 bits immediately following sync. It carries the Network Access Code (which network the traffic belongs to) and the Data Unit ID (DUID) — the frame's type. The DUID is what tells you "this is a data unit, not a voice frame," so you keep it and discard the rest.
Reconnaissance before decoding — "packet place awareness"
Because a location packet carries no voice, the only part that changes between two fixes is the GPS data itself. Record several keyups and difference them: the frame sync and NID are rock-constant, the header's sequence counters tick, and the data region varies. That difference map tells you where the overhead ends and the payload begins — before you have decoded a single field. (One caveat: interleaving spreads the varying bits across the data block, so the raw-symbol difference localizes the data region, not individual latitude vs. longitude characters. Those separate out one stage later, after de-interleaving.)

The data packet — headers, blocks, and the FEC stack

Past the sync and NID, a P25 data unit is a Packet Data Unit (PDU): one header block followed by some number of data blocks, all wrapped in layers of error-correction. The header describes the packet; the data blocks carry the payload; the FEC guarantees the payload survives a noisy channel intact — or is thrown away. There is no middle ground, and that is a feature (see the callout).

The header block

The header is a fixed 12-octet structure (before coding) that answers the receiver's questions about the packet. The fields that matter to a Tier 1 decoder are:

The SAP is the whole game
Service Access Point is how P25 multiplexes many kinds of data onto one air interface. General packet data that carries IP (the path that moves text messaging and vendor location) rides a different SAP — the one a general decoder keys on to reassemble IP/UDP datagrams. Tier 1 location uses its own dedicated Location Service SAP. That one field is both the thing that identifies our packets and the proof that they are not on the IP path: with a dedicated SAP and a data-header-offset of zero, the payload that follows the header is the NMEA itself — there is no IP header, no UDP header, nothing to unwrap. This is what makes Tier 1 strictly simpler to decode than general P25 packet data.

Data blocks and delivery mode

Data blocks come in two flavors. Confirmed blocks carry a sequence number and block CRC so the receiver can acknowledge each one and request retries — robust, but it needs a two-way conversation. Unconfirmed blocks are pure payload: no per-block sequence, no acknowledgement, fire and forget. Tier 1 uses unconfirmed delivery, and the choice is deliberate and correct: a GPS fix is perishable. By the time a retransmission handshake completed, the position would be stale; better to send a fresh fix than to guarantee an old one. Unconfirmed delivery also matches the operational model perfectly — the fix rides when the operator keys up, exactly when their location is most worth knowing.

The FEC stack

Between the raw symbols and the clean payload bytes sits a stack of error-correcting codes. Each exists to let the packet survive a channel that flips bits, and each is a published, invertible transform:

  • Trellis coding — a convolutional code (rate ½ for unconfirmed data) decoded with a Viterbi-style search. This is the heaviest lifter and the reason a wrong implementation produces pure garbage rather than "nearly right" output.
  • Interleaving — the coded bits of a block are scattered in a fixed pattern so that a burst of channel noise damages many independent code words a little, instead of one code word fatally. You undo it by applying the inverse permutation.
  • Golay, Hamming, and Reed–Solomon codes — block codes protecting specific fields (the header especially) with the ability to correct a bounded number of errors outright.
  • CRC — a final checksum over the assembled payload (a 32-bit packet CRC). It does not correct; it verdicts. Either the reassembled bytes match the CRC exactly, or the packet is rejected.
Why "nearly decoded" does not exist
Error-correction plus a final CRC makes decoding binary. The FEC either corrects every channel error and the CRC passes — in which case the payload is bit-perfect and the latitude is exactly right — or it fails and the packet is discarded. There is no partially-correct fix. For a decoder builder this is a gift: the CRC is an unforgeable pass/fail, so a correct decode proves itself and a wrong one cannot masquerade as success.

The Tier 1 payload — GGA and the $PP25 sentence

Undo the FEC, strip the pad and CRC, and what remains — for a Tier 1 location packet — is plain ASCII text: one or more NMEA 0183 sentences. This is the moment the whole chain pays off. The exotic radio protocol terminates in the same humble GPS format a hobbyist reads off a USB puck.

NMEA in sixty seconds

An NMEA sentence is a single line of printable ASCII: a $, a two-letter talker ID, a three-letter sentence type, a series of comma-separated fields, then * and a two-hex-digit checksum, terminated by carriage-return/line-feed. The checksum is simply the XOR of every character between the $ and the *. Fields may be left empty (two commas with nothing between) but the commas always hold their place, so you parse by counting commas, not columns. There is no handshake in NMEA itself — the receiver checks the checksum and silently ignores anything that fails, assuming a fresh line is coming.

What Tier 1 actually sends

The standard allows a family of NMEA sentences but makes exactly one mandatory on the transmitting side: GGA — "Global Positioning System Fix Data." That is the sentence that carries the essentials: time, latitude, longitude, fix quality, altitude. The others (GLL, GSA, GSV, RMA, VTG) are optional to send and mandatory to accept. In practice, a location packet is a GGA — optionally followed by more.

The unit identifier — the $PP25 sentence

A GGA tells you where, but not who. Tier 1 solves that with an optional proprietary NMEA sentence appended in the same packet, immediately after the GGA's line terminator. The convergence that makes validation trivial
A base radio that relays received fixes over a data link emits exactly this pair — $GNGGA for the position and $PP25 for the unit ID. That relay output is not a vendor convenience; it is the same standardized payload the radio pulled off the air. So a working over-the-air decoder and the radio's own relay must produce byte-identical lines. That gives a decoder builder something rare and powerful: a ground-truth answer to check against, character for character. More on this in §9.

The complete decode pipeline

Assemble the preceding sections and the full operation is a straight line of deterministic stages. Nothing in it is a guess; every box is a published transform with a known inverse.

Note what is not in the pipeline. There is no vocoder — that block exists only to turn voice frames into audio, and we discard voice. (Avoiding it also sidesteps the one patent-encumbered corner of P25 decoding, which guards voice, not data.) There is no SDR, no RF tuning, no channel-chasing — the radio sits on one channel and feeds us baseband continuously. And there is no IP/UDP reassembly, because the dedicated SAP and zero header-offset put the NMEA directly in the payload. The job is smaller than a general P25 decoder, not larger.

Validation — the known-answer method

Building a multi-stage decoder blind is miserable; a single wrong permutation in the interleaver produces output indistinguishable from noise, with no hint of where the error is. The way to make it tractable is to decode against a known answer.

A base radio that already relays received fixes gives you exactly that. Put it and the receiver on the same bench: the receiver's discriminator feeds the sound card (our decoder's input), while the base radio's relay output provides the finished NMEA for the very same transmission (the answer). Now every stage of the decoder can be checked against ground truth, and — because the relay output and the over-air payload are the same standardized bytes — a correct decode must match it character for character.

Two distinct checks fall out of this arrangement, and both are worth keeping:

  • The byte diff is the microscope. While the decoder is still being built, a mismatch points at the exact character that is wrong, which points at the stage that is wrong. This is how you climb the FEC stack without flying blind.
  • The map plot is the final proof. The real goal is to run the decoder with the reference feed disconnected and still watch the fix land on the correct position. A correct dot on the map, sourced purely from discriminator audio, is the whole objective realized: location off the air with nothing but a receiver and a sound card.

Timing, capacity, and operational behavior

A Tier 1 fix is small and quick. The data packet is deliberately capped so that a location report never monopolizes the channel. The practical envelope:

Representative Tier 1 location packet sizes and on-air durations. A GPS fix lives at the small end of what P25 can carry.
Update typePayloadApprox. airtime
Minimum GPS update~24 bytes~175 ms
Typical consumer-GPS sentence~71 bytes~300 ms
Maximum standard NMEA~83 bytes~325 ms
NMEA + unit-ID / user data~128 bytes~385 ms

The application payload is capped at 128 octets precisely to keep airtime down; encryption, where used, adds only tens of milliseconds. The operational consequences follow directly from the unconfirmed, PTT-shaped design:

  • No channel hog. A few hundred milliseconds per fix means location rides alongside voice without a dedicated data channel.
  • Freshness over guarantee. With no acknowledgement, a lost fix is simply not re-sent as an old value; a radio may re-transmit a stale fix a small number of times or on request, but the design prefers a new fix to a confirmed old one.
  • Capacity is an interval trade. Because many radios may transmit unconfirmed on the same channel, reliable delivery falls as the number of units and the update rate rise. Longer update intervals support more units at a given reliability — a planning dial, not a protocol limit. For a fire-ground model where a fix rides on each PTT (a mayday, an arrival, a size-up), the natural human cadence keeps the load low.
