The 60-second version

What this is, in plain English

Public-safety radios on the P25 standard can broadcast their GPS location right along with voice. Normally, reading those locations takes a specific manufacturer's base station feeding a special app — a walled garden.

This project tears the wall down. A radio tuned to the channel already turns the signal into a wiggle of voltage — the detector (discriminator) output. We take that wiggle into a $5 USB sound card and let software do the rest: find the digital symbols, rebuild the data, and read out the exact position and unit number the radio sent — straight off the air, from any compatible receiver, with no SDR and nobody's permission.

It started as one person's long-chased goal: get firefighters' locations off their radios so no one is lost in the woods or the smoke. That's still the point.

The part that changes everything

01Your receiver is in a dumpster

On January 1, 2013, the FCC's narrowbanding mandate took effect: every VHF and UHF land-mobile licensee had to cut their channels from the old 25 kHz "wideband" down to 12.5 kHz. Overnight, a mountain of perfectly good wideband two-way radios became non-compliant to license — and got stripped out of trucks, towers, and fire stations by the thousands.

They stopped being legal to transmit on.
They never stopped being excellent receivers.

That surplus is exactly the front end this project wants: a real commercial VHF radio you can set wide, tap at the detector, and feed to a sound card. They turn up for near-nothing — or free — at agencies, surplus sales, hamfests, and yes, the scrap pile. The expensive part of a software receiver was always the radio in front of it. Narrowbanding made that part free.

"Why not just use an SDR dongle?"

02Because a real radio can actually hear

The obvious move is a $30 SDR dongle. For a quiet bench, fine. But public safety doesn't live on a quiet bench — it lives next to high-power transmitters, repeater sites, pagers, broadcast towers, and a city full of RF. That environment is exactly where a purpose-built land-mobile radio (LMR) leaves a cheap SDR in the dust, and the reason is selectivity — the ability to hear one channel and reject everything else.

Purpose-built LMR radio

Built to hear one channel in a storm

  • Tuned front end / helical resonators — band-limited before the first mixer, so out-of-band giants (FM broadcast, pagers, cell, the repeater next to it) are rejected up front.
  • Crystal & ceramic IF filters — steep, multi-pole skirts giving 70–80+ dB adjacent-channel rejection. It pulls the wanted 12.5 kHz out clean even with a strong signal one channel over.
  • Superhet with high blocking dynamic range — engineered to survive co-located transmitters. A strong neighbor doesn't flatten it.
Typical cheap SDR dongle

Hears everything at once — and chokes

  • Wide-open broadband tuner (tens of MHz to GHz) with little or no preselection — it sees the whole spectrum, strong signals included.
  • Low-bit ADC, limited dynamic range — one strong nearby transmitter overloads and desenses the front end: intermod, images, phantom signals, and the weak signal you wanted is buried.
  • Filtering happens in software — too late. Once a blocker has clipped the converter, no amount of DSP recovers it. You can't un-saturate an ADC.

The honest version: a high-end SDR with an external preselector and a better converter can be superb — this isn't "SDR bad." It's that a free surplus LMR radio already has decades of RF front-end engineering baked in for precisely the hostile, transmitter-dense world public safety operates in. For this job, the radio in the dumpster isn't just cheaper than the dongle — in a dirty RF environment, it hears better.

How it works

03Detector audio in, map out

Tap the detector. A wire from the radio's detector/discriminator output (e.g. a rear accessory pin) into a USB sound card. The radio does the hard RF part; we take the recovered baseband.
Recover the symbols. Software locks onto the P25 four-level signal, finds the frame sync, and reads the digital bits — the same job a decoder chip does, done in plain code.
Rebuild the message. Error-correction (Viterbi, Golay, Hamming) repairs the bits, and out comes the standardized location sentence — position + the unit's own ID — and, from the voice frames, the audio itself.
Put it on a map. Each unit plots on an offline map by its ID; the talking unit can light up as it speaks. Nothing installed on the viewer, nothing in the cloud.
Where it stands — honestly

04What's actually proven

No hand-waving. Every claim here was checked against ground truth — the exact data a cooperating base reports — before it was believed.

  • GPS, live off the air. A transmission decoded the instant it keyed up — full position, valid checksum, correct unit ID — with no base radio, no SDR, no gateway. On a clean capture, 10 of 10 transmissions decoded exact (position, unit ID, and checksum) against the base's own feed.
  • Sensitivity matching the pros. Measured on a calibrated service monitor, the software receiver reaches its error limit at ≈ −119 dBm — inside the range of purpose-built commercial P25 hardware.
  • Voice, in free software. The same decoder recovers the voice frames and reconstructs intelligible audio — the clip at the top of this page came out of a real transmission, confirmed by ear by a 37-year radio engineer. (An independent vocoder cross-check is the next step to make it bulletproof.)
  • Runs on nearly nothing. A plain PC, or even a $40 travel router. Offline. No subscription, no vendor, no gatekeeper.

Receive only. This is a passive receiver — it only ever listens, and only to unencrypted traffic that a scanner could already hear. It decodes nothing that's encrypted and transmits nothing. Monitoring laws vary by place and use; it's on you to know and follow the rules where you are. This is a tool and a technique, shared openly for safety and learning — not anyone's data.
